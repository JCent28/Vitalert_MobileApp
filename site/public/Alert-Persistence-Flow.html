When Vitalert calls a nurse

Two rules decide when a nurse gets called to a patient. The first rule waits 15 seconds, to check that a bad reading is real and not just noise. The second rule counts how many times the patient has gone bad. Both rules run on the server, in one place.

WARNING at 15s

Warning and Critical both wait 15 seconds. No study has tested giving them different waits, so using two different numbers would mean we picked one ourselves instead of citing research.

If a panelist asks why a Warning does not wait longer, here is the answer. Warning sits right next to Normal: an SpO₂ of 94 is a Warning and 95 is Normal. So a reading that moves by one point can flip back and forth between them. Critical readings sit further away, so they flip less. That is a fair reason to make Warning wait longer, but no study has tested it. So both stay at 15 seconds, and the rule that needs three good readings in a row to clear an alert deals with the flipping instead.

The timer starts on the first bad reading, whether it is a Warning or a Critical, and runs the full 15 seconds. When the alert finally appears, it uses whichever level the reading is at that moment. Why 15 seconds and not 3, and not 2 minutes

McGrath and colleagues (2022) tested waits from 0 to 15 seconds. A 15-second wait removed 71% of the SpO₂ alarms and about 80% of the pulse rate alarms, because most false alarms stop on their own before 15 seconds is up. Rheineck-Leyssius and Kalkman (1998) tested waits from 2 to 44 seconds and got about 82% at 18 seconds.

15 is a number a real study tested and published a result for, so it can be cited directly instead of guessed from a range. For comparison: the panel asked for 1 to 2 minutes, and the developer built 3 seconds.

Part 2 · What the system checks on every reading

These same seven checks run every time the device sends a reading. Nothing else decides whether an alert appears.

  1. Is this a real reading?If BPM or SpO₂ is 0, stop here. Change nothing, and leave both counters alone.
  2. Label it.Normal, Warning or Critical, using the number ranges already in determineHealthStatus.
  3. Bad reading? Set the good-reading count back to 0.Any bad reading wipes out progress toward clearing the alert.
  4. Bad reading, and the timer is not running? Start it.Save the time of this first bad reading. Warning or Critical, it makes no difference. If the timer is already running, leave it alone, even if the reading changes from Warning to Critical.
  5. Has it been bad for 15 seconds?If not, do nothing and wait for the next reading. If yes, and there is no alert open yet, create the alert and add 1 to the counter.
  6. Good reading? Add 1 to the good-reading count.Below 3, nothing happens. The alert stays exactly as it is.
  7. Three good readings in a row? Stop the timer and close the alert.A Warning closes by itself. A Critical is marked as recovered but stays open until a nurse confirms it.
A Critical reading still skips ahead

If a patient already has a Warning alert and a Critical reading comes in, the alert upgrades to Critical right away. It does not start a new 15-second timer. triggerPhysicalAlert already does this upgrade, so the new timer just has to not block it.

The counter does not exist yet

Nothing on the Dashboard or the Alerts page counts alerts for each patient. The only count in the app is on the Patient Log page, the big number labelled “Total Alerts, in this session”, and it is not counting alerts. PatientLogController goes through the list of readings and adds 1 for every reading marked Critical or Warning. It never looks at the alerts saved in Firebase at all.

Since a reading is saved about once a second, a total of 47 on that card really means about 47 seconds of bad readings. The 15-second wait will not fix that number, because it was never counting alerts in the first place. That code has to be rewritten to count alerts instead.

Two different things to watch for

Both are counted from alerts, not from readings. A run of bad readings that made one alert counts as 1, no matter how long it lasted.

A patient who goes bad and will not recover is caught by the 15-second wait. The alert appears and then stays open until somebody deals with it.

A patient who keeps going bad is caught by the counter. Three separate alerts in one session, with a recovery in between each one, is a different problem from one long bad stretch, and it needs a different answer.

The gap this leaves, and where it is closed

A patient who goes Critical and stays Critical makes one alert, not three. The counter never reaches 3, the card never fills up, and going by the counter alone the nurse is never told to go. The sickest patient would be the one the rule never acts on.

Part 5 fixes this. One alert that nobody has dealt with for 60 seconds turns the counter red by itself, with no need for a second or third alert.

Three more decisions the developer will need

What does a card look like at 1 or 2 alerts?The nurse is not told to go until the third alert, but the first two alerts still exist. The card probably appears with one or two rows in it and no message.
What happens at 4 and beyond?One card holds 3 alerts. Does the fourth alert start a second card, so a patient with 7 alerts ends up with three cards? The panel said the count keeps going up past 3.
Does the order of the levels change the gradient?W,C,W and C,W,W have the same two levels, just in a different order. The simplest rule is: all three the same means a solid colour, any mix means a gradient from warning to critical, and the order is ignored. That is easier to build and easier to read.

Part 5 · Two ways the nurse gets told to go

Both ways end up showing the nurse the same thing: a red counter and a message. So there is one result, reached in two different ways.

The patient who goes bad and stays bad
0sSpO₂ drops to 85. The timer starts.

16–74sStill 85. No recovery, so no second alert. Counter stays at 1.

Without this path the counter would sit at 1 for the whole session and the nurse would never be called.

The patient who keeps going bad

Each alert here is a separate bad stretch with a recovery in between, which is why they count separately.

The one rule that changes

The number alone no longer decides the colour. It turns red when the counter reaches 3, or when an alert has sat for 60 seconds without anyone dealing with it. Without the message underneath, a nurse seeing a red 1 would have no idea why.

What drives the 60-second timer

Nothing new is needed. Readings come in every few seconds. Each time one arrives, the server checks whether this patient has an open alert older than 60 seconds that has not been flagged yet, and flags it. Same function, same place, no scheduled job and no extra program.

If the device stops sending readings entirely, nothing runs the check, but the idle detection already in the app handles a disconnected device separately.

McGrath's system also pages the nurse again every 3 minutes while the patient is still bad. That would mean a repeat chime on the same schedule. Worth leaving out for now and putting in Recommendations instead.

Part 6 · Where it goes in the code

One rule, on the server. The copies in the simulator and in the browser get deleted.

PatientController::sendToFirebase The 15-second wait goes here, just before triggerPhysicalAlert is called. This is the only code that both the real HDA1 and the simulator pass through.
patients/{id} in Firebase Stores what the timer needs: when the bad readings started, what level they are, how many good readings in a row, and how many alerts this session. Each reading arrives as its own separate web request, so none of this can be kept in memory.
PatientController::startSession Reset the timer and the counter, in the same block that already sets final_vitals to null.
PatientController::endSession Reset both again, in the $patientUpdates array.
AlertController::acknowledge Clear the counter when a nurse verifies the patient. This is the panel's own reset rule.
PatientLogController — the alertCounts loop Rewrite. Right now it goes through $readings and counts the bad readings while calling them alerts. It needs to count the session's actual alerts instead, or the Patient Log will keep showing seconds of bad readings as an alert count.
dashboard/index.blade.php and alerts/index.blade.php Build. Neither page shows an alert count for each patient today, only the level badges. The live counter the panel asked for has to be added here, where the nurse is actually looking.
SimulateDeviceVitals::evaluateAlerts Delete. Its 3 and 6 second timers are a second, different opinion about when to alarm, and everything it remembers is lost when the program stops.
notification-manager.js — evaluateVitalWindow Delete. A timer that lives inside one browser tab empties when the page is refreshed, and disappears completely when the tab is closed.
notification-manager.js — the alerts listener Keep. Once every alert in Firebase has already passed the 15-second wait, chiming the moment one appears is correct. Add a second chime when an existing alert changes level, so an upgrade is still heard.

Where these numbers came from

The 15 seconds comes straight from McGrath and colleagues, who tested that exact wait and published the result, so it can be cited directly instead of guessed. But nothing was tested on the HDA1 itself, and neither study checked whether any real emergencies got missed. So the paper can say 15 seconds is what the research supports. It cannot say the wait is safe.

The 3 good readings needed to clear an alert has no study behind it at all. Neither paper tested how an alarm should clear. That number is our own choice, and the paper should say so plainly. The counter threshold of 3 is not research either, it came from the panel.

Chapter 4 reports how fast alerts reached the nurse, measured back when there was no 15-second wait at all. Those numbers now describe a device that no longer works that way, so the write-up needs a line saying the wait was added after the testing was done, in response to the panel.
